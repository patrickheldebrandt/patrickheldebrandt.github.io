March 11

a. Prediction vs. Explanation

Prediction focuses on accurately forecasting outcomes, while explanation focuses on understanding the underlying causal mechanisms. In economics and public policy, this distinction matters because a model can predict well without explaining why something occurs.

b. Causal Diagram and Causal Inference

State Partisanship → Probability of AI Bill Enactment

Potential confounders include:

  • state economic development,

  • technology sector size,

  • public concern about AI,

  • lobbying activity,

  • legislative professionalism.

These factors may influence both state partisanship and the likelihood of AI legislation passing.

A simple causal diagram would be:

State Economic Conditions → State Partisanship
State Economic Conditions → AI Bill Enactment
State Partisanship → AI Bill Enactment

To distinguish a causal claim from a predictive association, it is necessary to control for confounding variables and establish temporal ordering. A predictive model may show that Democratic-controlled states are more likely to pass AI legislation, but this alone does not prove causality. A causal analysis would require stronger identification strategies, such as fixed effects, quasi-experimental designs, or controlling for omitted variable bias, to isolate the independent effect of partisanship on legislative outcomes.

March 31

b. Strengths and Failures of LLMs

Large Language Models (LLMs) perform well at tasks involving text synthesis, summarization, coding assistance, pattern recognition, and organizing large amounts of information. In research settings, they are particularly useful for generating drafts, cleaning code, explaining statistical concepts, and accelerating literature reviews. However, they still struggle with reliability and factual consistency.

One important failure mode I have encountered is hallucination. While working on legislative data collection, the model occasionally generated incorrect author information or inferred political affiliations that were not explicitly present in the source material. This becomes problematic in research because fabricated information can easily contaminate datasets and reduce validity. Hallucinations are especially dangerous when the output appears confident and academically written. As a result, I found that human verification and source checking remain essential when using LLMs for empirical research.

a. Generative AI and the Future of Economics Research

Over the next five years, generative AI will likely reshape economics by automating many time-intensive research tasks. Literature reviews, coding, data cleaning, text analysis, and preliminary model construction will become significantly faster. In policy research, LLMs may also improve the ability to process unstructured data such as legislation, news articles, and public comments at scale.

The tasks that will change most are repetitive analytical workflows and early-stage exploratory research. Researchers will spend less time manually cleaning datasets or debugging code and more time interpreting results and designing research questions.

However, causal reasoning, research design, ethical judgment, and interpretation of findings still require human oversight. Economists must determine whether a model’s conclusions are theoretically meaningful and methodologically credible. AI can assist with analysis, but human researchers are still necessary to evaluate validity, contextualize results, and make normative policy judgments.

April 7

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is an AI framework that combines large language models with external document retrieval systems. Instead of relying only on the model’s internal training data, RAG first retrieves relevant documents, databases, or sources and then uses that information to generate a response.

Grounding generation in retrieved documents improves trustworthiness because responses can be tied directly to evidence rather than generated purely from statistical patterns in the model. In research settings, this reduces hallucinations and increases transparency, since users can often trace claims back to source materials.

However, recent criticisms of RAG highlight several limitations. First, retrieval quality is often inconsistent; if the system retrieves irrelevant or incomplete documents, the generated response may still be misleading. Second, RAG systems can struggle with reasoning across multiple documents or resolving conflicting information. Finally, many RAG systems still generate confident-sounding answers even when retrieved evidence is weak.

April 14

a. Knowledge Graphs and Knowledge Mining

A knowledge graph is a structured representation of information that stores entities and the relationships between them. Unlike a traditional relational database, which mainly stores data in tables with predefined schemas, a knowledge graph emphasizes connections and semantic relationships across data points. A vector store, by comparison, focuses on embedding-based similarity search and is primarily designed for retrieving semantically similar text rather than explicitly modeling relationships.

Relationships between entities are valuable for knowledge mining because they allow researchers to analyze networks, dependencies, and patterns that are difficult to observe in flat datasets. Knowledge graphs make it possible to connect people, organizations, policies, and events in a way that better reflects real world interactions. This is especially useful in policy and legislative research, where outcomes are often shaped by interconnected political and institutional factors.

b. Knowledge Graph Example from AI Legislation Research

In my research domain, three possible entities are:

  • AI Bill

  • State Legislature

  • Bill Sponsor

Three relationships could include:

  • introduced_in → connects an AI bill to a state legislature

  • sponsored_by → connects a bill to a legislator

  • belongs_to_party → connects a sponsor to a political party

This structure would allow more complex questions to be answered than a flat dataset alone. For example, a knowledge graph could identify which political networks are most associated with successful AI legislation, trace collaborations between sponsors across states, or examine how party affiliation influences specific categories of AI policy. A flat dataset may store the same variables, but it is less effective at capturing interconnected relationships and multi-step queries across entities.

April 21

AI for Science and Agentic AI Systems

“AI for Science” refers to the use of artificial intelligence to accelerate scientific discovery, automate research tasks, and assist with analyzing complex data. Rather than simply generating text, AI systems can support hypothesis generation, literature synthesis, simulation, coding, and large-scale pattern detection across scientific domains.

Agentic AI systems extend these capabilities by incorporating planning, tool use, and multi-step reasoning. Unlike single-prompt interactions, where the model responds once to a user query, agentic systems can break problems into stages, retrieve external information, execute tools such as coding environments or databases, evaluate intermediate outputs, and iteratively refine results.
